# AI Safety And Understanding How Things Work

I recently saw someone making this argument about whether it's important to understand how things made with LLMs work in order to use them:

https://x.com/AndrewCurran_/status/2108992955382210632

For those who don't want to go to Twitter, it shows a slide by Terrence Tao that says:

> A thought experiment on alignment and understanding
> 
> • Suppose an advanced Al is prompted to "find a cure for cancer that passes a stage 3 clinical trial".
> 
> • After a large amount of compute, it produces a cocktail of previously unknown chemicals which its mathematical model predicts, when mixed and injected into a patient, will kill all their cancer cells.
> 
> • While nobody truly knows how this cocktail was found, this model prediction is confirmed in Lean, and the cocktail indeed passes a stage 3 trial.
> 
> • Could the Al solution to the prompt be somehow misaligned by exploiting a weakness in the trial process?
> 
> • Before injecting this cocktail into your bloodstream, would you want to know that there is at least one human cancer expert who understands the mechanism behind this cure?
> 
> • Or a human mathematician who understands the mathematical model used to locate the cocktail?

The comment was:

> This is a slide from Terence Tao's 'Math 2.0' public lecture yesterday evening at Caltech. I am highly confident that, if the cure actually worked, almost any Stage 4 cancer patient would tell you that human comprehension of its mechanism is completely irrelevant to them.

Leaving the circular logic aside, there's something else that I think is pretty important to talk about here.

## Understanding vs knowing what'll happen

The concept of only using things you fully understand the function of is a red herring. People have been using flint stones without understanding quantum physics for 3.3 million years, it is a lynchpin of our civilization. What you want is understanding of *consequences*.

## Simple machines vs complex machines

The thing is, a piece of flint is a very simple thing. It can do a few tricks, some which we discovered in the stone age, and to make it do other tricks you have to do some very unnatural combinations of steps that don't come up naturally. This ensures safety.

Complex machines are different. If you went back in time and handed a stone age person a loaded gun, they would be liable to hurt themselves. We know this because of incidents involving children who do not understand guns or death yet. They don't know the consequences.

## Limitations of human intelligence

Our immediate, in-the-moment intelligence can only work with continuous transitions: it can only predict outcomes which are [not that far from the current situation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_of_a_function#(%CE%B5,_%CE%B4)-definition_of_limit). That's why we have the concept of surprise. With a flint, any surprise is limited in scope. You might cut yourself, or burn something down.

We cannot innately predict through intelligence outcomes like explosion, fission, motion with acceleration, electricity, etc. We can only "predict it" via fact recall - recalling a previous situation or something we had been taught.

For all but the simplest things, we are like children handed a loaded gun. We shouldn't be going looking for these. 90 year olds with cancer have a simpler choice - the reasoning is "I will die anyways, but this might help". We have an infinite supply of end-stage patients.

Most situations are not end-stage, and treating normal life like a cancer patient with nothing to lose is, let's be honest, kinda stupid. Most people's lives, most businesses and organisations have a lot to lose on an untested strategy that "might work" or could lose it all.

If you leave a child alone in a room with a piece of flint, they're certainly going to be safe. Replace that with a gun, and you're making bad choices. AI is more complex in outcomes than other technology, it's unpredictable.

Let's not be children playing with a loaded gun.
